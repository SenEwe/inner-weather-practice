# Inner Weather Practice – At Home Within Yourself

Recognizing how the events of your day shape your inner weather.  
The cognitive connection between observation and everyday life.

> The Inner Weather Practice consists of two simple parts:  
>   
> **FAC  
> Three questions** throughout the day:  
> **Friendliness · Attentiveness · Composure**  
>   
> **HBM**  
> **Five minutes** of sitting every evening:  
> **Head · Body · Mood**  
>   
> No technique. No teachers.  
> Just you. Your everyday life. And time.

## 1. Friendliness

Imagine that, for the rest of your life, you did nothing more than keep returning to one single word: **FRIENDLINESS.**

What kind of progress would that be?

It might begin with simple questions. How is friendliness defined on Wikipedia? How is it understood in English? In French? In Spanish?

Then, little by little, you begin to notice what friendliness actually is.  
In the way you meet other people. And, from time to time, in the way you relate to yourself.   
  
Can you feel the difference? When you are unfriendly towards someone else… What does it create?  
How does it feel when you first become unfriendly towards yourself?

Is that really the direction you want your life to take?  
Or, if you knew how, would you rather move a little more towards friendliness? Isn’t it sometimes necessary to be unfriendly? Isn’t everyone like that? Or do I really have to answer unfriendliness with more unfriendliness?

Is there a way to remain friendly towards yourself and towards others — even, and perhaps especially, in difficult situations?  
  
Would that not open doors that would otherwise remain closed?  
Wouldn’t friendliness make life easier, in general?  
What would that be like?

This is the beginning. And you may stay here.  
For as long as you need.  
For as long as you feel it is necessary.

## 2. Attentiveness

Here the bodily aspect becomes important.

When you are inattentive, the only way back into an attentive mode is through bodily sensation. An exam blackout, for example.

In everyday life, too, you begin to notice more and more that it is often about those moments of inattention that arise below conscious awareness. The moments when you actually want to be somewhere else entirely.

To notice how powerful this process is, and slowly begin to understand its connections.

Over months, an increasingly continuous attentiveness can be cultivated in this way. Through positive reinforcement. By noticing again and again that it feels better to remain attentive — and that this also allows you to remain capable of acting in unpleasant situations.

But also by noticing when you simply cannot go on anymore. When you really do not want to know anything about life for a while, and in doing so actually train inattention — through distraction, diversion, and many other things.

Slowly accepting this, and then becoming accustomed, in small steps, to attentiveness in life — in friendly moments.

Step by step, life becomes noticeably more pleasant.

You become healthier.

## 3. Composure

Composure is bodily to the highest degree.

It is pure relaxation.

When you are under stress, caught in an exam blackout, or surrounded by the noise of everyday life, intellectual insight does not help.

What you need then is the bodily instance of knowing:

What exactly does this constriction feel like?

And what is the way back into sinking and letting go?

Only then does self-regulation return.

Perhaps you will even manage the exam after all.

To notice, again and again, that composure can only be allowed to arise — and how tension appears again and again, all by itself, in response to the demands of everyday life:

That is the path.

The body remembers the way.

And so composure begins to arise more and more often.

Yes, you can also explore composure intellectually.

That can be helpful too.

But in the end, the only truly sustainable access is through the body.

If you first have to feed your mind with a theory or worldview before you are able to find inner calm →

## Module 1: Deconstruction

### Dissolving Static Thing-Thinking

**Intention:**  
To recognize how deeply our language, culture, and social structures have trained us to divide the world into supposedly fixed objects, isolated egos, and instrumental pressures of optimization.

**Hartmut Rosa:** ***Resonance: A Sociology of Our Relationship to the World***

**The intellectual lever:**  
Rosa provides the sociological diagnosis of the modern mode of exhaustion. He shows how modernity pressures us to relate to the world instrumentally — through availability, control, mastery, and efficiency — thereby eroding living relationship itself. Only against this background does it become clear why a shift into the Somatic Resource Mode can become an existential necessity.

**Heinz von Foerster:** ***Truth Is the Invention of a Liar***

**The intellectual lever:**  
Foerster breaks the illusion of a “static world out there” that we merely represent passively. Radically and humorously, he shows that knowing is a circular, cybernetic process.

**Alfred North Whitehead:** ***Science and the Modern World*** **(1925)**

**The intellectual lever:**  
Especially important is his discussion of the **fallacy of misplaced concreteness**. Once this is understood, it becomes immediately clear why we so often freeze the flow of life into apparently fixed “things.”

**Alan Watts:** ***The Book: On the Taboo Against Knowing Who You Are***

**The intellectual lever:**  
Watts brilliantly connects Western process philosophy with Eastern thought. He deconstructs the feeling of the “isolated ego” and shows that the human being is not an object placed inside the world, but a process the whole universe is enacting here and now.

## Module 2: Reconditioning

### Learning to Recognize Patterns, Feedback, and Flow

**Intention:**  
To train the mind to loosen its attachment to seemingly fixed reality and begin thinking in loops, relations, physical flows, and dynamic equilibria.

**Donella Meadows:** ***Thinking in Systems: A Primer***

**The intellectual lever:**  
This trains the eye for what SenGeKu calls the **Inner Weather**. Meadows shows how systems “breathe” through largely invisible flows, stocks, and feedback loops. Events are no longer understood linearly — as “A causes B” — but as the movement of interacting patterns.

**Gregory Bateson:** ***Steps to an Ecology of Mind*** **(1972)**

**The intellectual lever:**  
Bateson anchors the principle that mind and information are not material objects, but relational processes — famously, “a difference that makes a difference.” Essential reading for dissolving the apparent separation between mind and nature.

**David Bohm:** ***Wholeness and the Implicate Order***

**The intellectual lever:**  
A fundamental bridge into quantum physics. Bohm challenges the atomistic image of reality as a collection of isolated particles and develops instead the idea of an undivided, flowing movement — the **holomovement** — from which matter and organisms emerge as temporary forms or vortices.

---

## Module 3: Embodying the Process

### From System to Living Organism

**Intention:**  
To build the bridge from systems thinking to the biology of the body and of nature — and to understand that “stability” and regeneration exist only through continuous activity, ongoing self-organization, and the reduction of instrumental interference.

**George L. Engel: “The Need for a New Medical Model: A Challenge for Biomedicine” (1977)**

**The intellectual lever:**  
A central text for biological self-understanding. Engel challenges the medical machine-model and describes the human being as an integrated biopsychosocial process. Nervous system, cells, psyche, and social environment form an interconnected whole. Exhaustion is therefore not merely the defect of a single organ, but a disturbance within the larger organization of life.

**Humberto Maturana & Francisco Varela:** ***The Tree of Knowledge***

**The intellectual lever:**  
An introduction to **autopoiesis** — self-production and self-maintenance. The book makes intellectually tangible that a living organism is not a finished product, but an ongoing circular process of regeneration. It prepares the mind to understand Quiet Sitting or slow movement not as techniques of optimization, but as ways of caring for this basic biological mode of self-organization.

**Masanobu Fukuoka:** ***The One-Straw Revolution*** **(1975)**

**The intellectual lever:**  
A radical practical expression of **Wu Wei** — minimal or non-forcing action. Fukuoka demonstrates, on the larger scale of nature, what Engel suggests for the organism: the regeneration of an exhausted system does not necessarily arise from doing more, but from systematically reducing unnecessary intervention and control. It mirrors the principle of non-performative dwelling practiced on the Dragon Meadow.

## Module 4: Integration

### The Philosophical Synthesis

**Intention:**  
To give the transformed relationship to world and body a stable intellectual and metaphysical foundation.

**Nicholas Rescher:** ***Process Metaphysics*** **(1996)**

**The intellectual lever:**  
Rescher provides a precise conceptual framework. He challenges classical substance metaphysics and argues that becoming is more fundamental than static being. This gives the practitioner a rigorous philosophical basis for understanding a world perceived increasingly in terms of process, relation, and flow.

# In the Evening: Observe for 5 Minutes

Every evening. At the same time.

### What you need:

- A chair
- 5 minutes
- Nothing else

### What you do:

- Sit down
- Do nothing
- Observe

No music. No knitting. No distraction. No phone.

Just three questions:

## What is happening **in my head**?

- Are thoughts circling?
- Is it calm?
- Is everything mixed up?

## What is **my body doing**?

- Tense?
- Soft?
- Tired?
- Restless?

## What is **my mood like**?

- Exhausted?
- Irritated?
- Sad?
- Neutral?
- Content?

You do not judge.  
You do not change anything.  
You simply look.

Like looking at the weather.

Is it raining inside you right now? Okay.  
Stormy? That is okay too.  
Sunny? Good.

**That is it.  
5 minutes.**

---

> **Not Longer!**  
> Do not play around with the duration. Do not keep extending it.  
> If you do, you **need a teacher**.  
> Even if you think you have already achieved something — that very thought may already be an obvious wrong turn.  
>   
> Every Evening!  
> **CONSISTENTLY.** So that it becomes a habit.

## What Changes — Over Months

This is not a trick.

It is a slow process.

### After 2 weeks:

You begin to notice at all how you actually feel.  
Before that, you may have been numb to it.

### After 2 months:

You begin to see patterns.  
When you are tense.  
When you are absent.

### After 6 months:

Your basic mode begins to shift.  
You are calm more often.  
Present more often.  
Friendly more often.

### After one year:

You live differently.  
Not because you are making an effort.  
But because you know yourself.

This is called the **Somatic Resource Mode** — a relaxed and effective basic mode.

But:

That is not the goal.  
It simply happens as you begin to perceive yourself more clearly.

> The Inner Weather Practice is the distilled entry practice of SenGeKu.  
>   
> It creates the conditions for patterns of thinking, feeling, and bodily action to be cultivated anew — no longer primarily from stress, but gradually from greater presence, friendliness, and composure.  
>   
> And that is Freundsamkeit — Somatic Resource Mode, lived.
